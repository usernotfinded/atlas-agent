### CAND-032 — Deferred domain loading for CLI startup

Every `atlas` invocation costs ~0.50 s before it does anything: ~346 ms
importing `atlas_agent.cli`, ~75 ms building the argparse tree, ~12 ms of
interpreter startup. `atlas --version` pays all of it.

This is primarily a developer-loop cost rather than a UX one. The suite
exercises the CLI the way an operator does, by spawning it, from 97 call sites;
the dev gate's slowest step spends 73% of its wall clock inside 21 such
subprocesses.

Two levers are measured and priced in
[safety-invariant-audit-followups.md](../development/safety-invariant-audit-followups.md).
`ConfigDict(defer_build=True)` takes 74% off pydantic class creation and about a
quarter of startup, but 128 of the 129 model classes inherit `BaseModel`
directly with no shared base to set it on, and it moves a malformed model from
an import-time error to a first-use error. Lazy per-domain handler import has a
larger ceiling — `config` alone imports in 137 ms against 273 ms for
`cli_commands` — but threads local imports through a 5.9k-line dispatch whose
command surface is a pinned trust contract. Choosing between them, or declining
both, is what the candidate is for.

#### What the lazy-import lever is actually worth

Measured, because the attributed numbers make it look far larger than it is.
`-X importtime` bills `atlas_agent.backtest` 193 ms as the first importer of
infrastructure the rest of the CLI graph loads anyway. Timing each module alone
against its cost once `config`, `execution`, `safety` and `brokers` are already
loaded:

| module | alone (attributed) | marginal |
|---|---|---|
| `atlas_agent.cli_commands` | 282 ms | 64 ms |
| `atlas_agent.backtest` | 206 ms | 25 ms |
| `atlas_agent.risk` | 188 ms | **0 ms** |
| `atlas_agent.audit` | 165 ms | **0 ms** |

`risk` and `audit` are worth nothing at all — they are entirely shared
infrastructure, and their three-figure attributions are artifacts of who
imported them first. Deferring **all four** of the largest imports has a ceiling
of about 89 ms against a measured 690 ms for `atlas --version` as a subprocess
(bare `python -c pass` is 16 ms on the same machine). That is roughly 13%, and
it is the optimistic bound: `cli_commands` is on the path of nearly every real
command, so its 64 ms is not free to defer.

This is the same trap already recorded for `urllib.request` in
[safety-invariant-audit-followups.md](../development/safety-invariant-audit-followups.md),
met again from the other end. The conclusion for this candidate is that lazy
per-domain import is not the larger lever it appeared to be, and threading local
imports through a 5.9k-line dispatch whose command surface is a pinned trust
contract buys at most 13% of one invocation.

The developer-loop argument does not rescue it either. Counting every
`subprocess.run` across a full suite run: **2,419 spawns totalling 485.7 s of
1,406 s — 35% of the suite.** That sounds like the lever's case until the mean
is checked. It is 201 ms per spawn against 690 ms for a cold `atlas --version`,
so most spawns are not cold CLI invocations at all; at most 704 of the 2,419
can be, since 704 × 690 ms already accounts for the whole 485.7 s. Applying the
89 ms ceiling to that upper bound gives **63 s, or 4.5% of the suite** — an
upper bound on an upper bound.

The remaining 65% of suite time is not subprocess at all. The split that
actually characterises it is tier: the quick tier averages 9 ms per test while
the non-quick tests average 266 ms, a 30x gap across 5,245 tests, and the 30
slowest tests are only 19% of the total. Whatever is worth optimising here is
distributed, and it is not import time.

Acceptance would require the command surface to be unchanged:
`scripts/check_cli_command_compatibility.py` passing, trust-contract envelopes
byte-identical, and a test pinning the measured reduction so it cannot silently
regress.

This is import-timing and packaging only. It changes no gate, no default, and no
runtime decision — not `RiskManager`, approval queues, the kill switch,
heartbeat, deadman, the audit hash-chain, live-submit opt-in, or `can_submit` —
loads no credentials, and adds no network access. `cli_bootstrap.py` keeps its
four configless commands and its stdlib-only module scope.

### CAND-033 — Wire the declared risk limits that no rule reads

Hard invariant 5 states that `RiskManager` enforces "hard-coded limits on
position size, notional, daily loss, exposure, symbols, and leverage". Four of
those six have rules. Two do not.

`max_daily_loss_pct` is declared in `RiskLimits` with a 2% default and is read by
no rule. `OrderRiskInput.leverage` is populated from the order on both the paper
and live-submit paths and is read by no rule. Measured against the defaults:

```
baseline, healthy portfolio          allowed=True  violations=[]
realized_pnl_today = -50% of equity  allowed=True  violations=[]
order leverage = 10x                 allowed=True  violations=[]
trades_today = 100 (limit says 5)    allowed=True  violations=[]
```

A third limit sits outside invariant 5 and is the worst of the three, because
the operator is shown it. `atlas risk check` prints exactly three lines — kill
switch, max position size, max trades per day. The first two are enforced.
`max_trades_per_day` is configured, defaults to 5, and `RiskLimits` has no such
field at all.

The daily-loss chain is a stub at both ends. `realized_pnl_today` travels from
`PortfolioState` through `PortfolioSnapshot` into every evaluation, and nothing
in `src/` ever increments it, so it is always zero. Adding the rule alone would
be a limit on a number nobody writes; closing it properly means deciding where
realized daily P&L is accounted — the paper broker's fills, the trade journal, or
a session boundary the caller resets. That is a portfolio-accounting decision
rather than a risk-rule one, which is why this is a candidate and not a fix.

Leverage is narrower. No broker adapter forwards the field, so nothing acts on it
today. The question is whether to reject leveraged orders at the gate or to stop
carrying a field the system does not use.

The trade-count limit is the readiest of the three: `trades_today` is already
incremented by the paper broker and already reaches every evaluation, so it needs
only a rule. It also has an option the others do not — stop `atlas risk check`
printing it, which removes the misleading claim without touching the gate.

`tests/risk/test_declared_limits_are_wired.py` states both behaviours as strict
`xfail` cases, so implementing either turns the marker into a failing XPASS that
has to be removed. The same file pins the four limits that do work.

This tightens the deterministic risk gate and enables nothing: no live trading,
live submit, broker or provider execution, credential loading, or network
access, and no default moves toward permissiveness. It can only reject orders
that are accepted today.

### CAND-034 — Map the research error codes the CLI flattens

`safe_research_session_error` maps a `ResearchSessionError` to a vetted status
and message, falling back to `("research_error", "Research command failed.")`
for anything it does not recognise. The fallback is the fail-safe half of the
design and stays: an unmapped exception string must never be echoed, because in
general it can carry a path or user data.

What is wrong is how often it fires. `src/atlas_agent` raises 205 distinct
*literal* codes; `RESEARCH_SESSION_ERROR_CODES` maps 69. So at least 136 land on
the generic message, and 30 of the 175 `atlas research` subcommands answer a
nonexistent id with "Research command failed." when the reason was known,
static, and safe to show — `provider_safety_dossier_source_seal_missing`, for
one. A further 33 raise sites build their code at runtime and are not counted, so
136 is a floor rather than a total.

The open question is whether all of them deserve a distinct message. Eleven
different not-found spellings already exist across the `-show` commands alone
(`not_found`, `artifact_not_found`, `research_artifact_not_found`,
`dossier_not_found`, `report_not_found`, and others). Adding that many strings
without settling the vocabulary first would make the surface harder to consume,
not easier.

`tests/research/test_research_error_code_mapping.py` ratchets that count so the
backlog cannot grow while the decision is pending, and fails if someone raises
the budget instead of lowering it.

This only widens a vetted allowlist. It enables nothing at runtime and changes
no gate, default, or trading behaviour.

#### The backlog is mostly not a wording task

"136 messages to word" is what makes this candidate look like a product decision.
Splitting the 139 unmapped literal codes by what each would actually require says
otherwise:

| group | count | what it needs |
|---|---|---|
| `*_not_found` (11), `*_missing` (2), `*_malformed` (9) | **22** | Nothing. An existing table entry already covers each, used verbatim by 11 and 10 precedents respectively. |
| `X_source_Y_missing` | **62** | The established per-artifact convention — its own code plus "Invalid \<thing\> artifact." Mechanical apart from the artifact's display name. |
| everything else | **55** | Genuine new wording. |

So 84 of 139 follow a convention the table already demonstrates, and the real
product decision is 55 strings, not 136. That also answers the open question above
in part: the eleven competing not-found spellings are a reason to route new
not-found codes to the *existing* `research_artifact_not_found`, which is what
every mapped precedent does, rather than to mint a twelfth.

One of the 22 is `provider_safety_dossier_missing`, which is what makes
`provider-safety-dossier-summary` refuse opaquely when handed the `run_id` its
own `--help` advertises — see the open item in
[safety-invariant-audit-followups.md](../development/safety-invariant-audit-followups.md).
Mapping that one code would not fix the mislabeled argument, but it would stop
the failure being unreadable.

Note what the split does not change: the status codes are machine-readable output
that consumers may already branch on, so moving a case from `research_error` to
`research_artifact_not_found` is still a contract change. Being mechanical is not
the same as being free, which is why this is measured here rather than applied.

### CAND-035 — Absorb the repeated research handler preamble

`cli_commands/research/` holds 170 handlers across 7314 statements, median 42
statements each. 166 of them open and close the same way: `resolve_workspace_path()`,
an identical `no_workspace` branch printing the same JSON, a `try`, an
`except ResearchSessionError` mapping through `safe_research_session_error`, and
an `except Exception` emitting `research_error`. Two handlers taken from
different modules are 52% line-identical, and 80 of the 170 share an AST shape
with another handler.

What makes this proposable now rather than earlier is the safety net.
`tests/research/test_research_command_envelopes.py` runs all 175 subcommands and
pins the envelope and the exit-status agreement, so a refactor of this size has
something to fail against beyond the unit tests of each handler.

The open question is whether the shared shape is one thing or several that
currently look alike. The 33 handlers that build their error code at runtime, and
the four whose exit status disagrees with their own envelope, are evidence the
family is less uniform than the line counts suggest. A wrapper that forces them
into one shape would either paper over those differences or need escape hatches
that reintroduce the duplication. Spiking one module and measuring the real
reduction should come before committing to 170.

#### Measurement, and the constraint it turned up

Measured by AST over `cli_commands/research/`: 170 handlers, of which 163 carry
the full canonical shape, and the absorbable scaffolding — dispatch guard,
workspace resolution and its `no_workspace` branch, the two canonical `except`
blocks, and the trailing `return None` — comes to **4,147 lines of 11,217, about
36%**. It is strikingly uniform: **123 handlers at exactly 25 such lines and 34
at exactly 24**, so 92% sit within one line of each other.

The uniformity extends further than the line counts suggest, in the opposite
direction to the worry above. 141 handlers have exactly the canonical two
`except` clauses. The other 24 do not depart from the shape; they *prepend*
more specific clauses — `InvalidResearchSymbolError`,
`UnsupportedArtifactSchemaError`, `UnsupportedResearchProviderError`,
`ResearchConfigurationError` — and still end with the canonical pair. A wrapper
supplying the outer pair leaves those free to keep an inner `try`, with ordering
preserved, so they need no escape hatch.

Part of that scaffolding is provably dead rather than merely repeated. Each
handler opens with `if args.command == "research" and args.research_command ==
"<name>":` and closes with `return None` for the non-match — but
`dispatch_research` looks the handler up in a `HANDLERS` dict keyed by exactly
that name, and `cli.py` only calls it under `if args.command == "research"`.
Checked across the package, **all 170 handlers' internal guard matches their own
registry key exactly**, so the branch can never be false and the trailing
`return None` can never be reached.

One constraint has to be respected, and it is the reason a naive wrapper would
regress the envelope silently. Those prepended clauses are **load-bearing, not
redundant**. `safe_research_session_error` is an exact dict lookup on `str(exc)`
against snake_case keys, while `sanitize_symbol` raises
`InvalidResearchSymbolError("Invalid research symbol.")` — prose. The lookup
misses, so an `InvalidResearchSymbolError` that reaches the generic path
degrades to `research_error`:

```
'Invalid research symbol.'  -> ('research_error', 'Research command failed.')
'invalid_research_symbol'   -> ('invalid_research_symbol', 'Invalid research symbol.')
```

Deleting a specific clause on the assumption that the generic path maps it the
same way would change that handler's status code and message while every unit
test of the wrapper still passed. The acceptance criterion that all 175
subcommands keep the same envelope is what would catch it, which is the argument
for landing the refactor only with that test in place.

#### The spike, and what it says about the safety net

One module was converted against a wrapper supplying the dispatch guard,
workspace resolution and the canonical error envelope, then reverted. It works:
`credential.py` went from **390 lines to 207 (-46%)**, the wrapper costs 125
lines once, and at 30.5 lines saved per handler it breaks even after 4.1 of the
170. Extrapolated, the migration is worth roughly **-5,060 net lines**.

The 24 handlers with prepended clauses need no escape hatch. The one in
`credential.py` keeps a three-line `try` around `sanitize_symbol` inside the
wrapped body, and because the wrapper's clauses sit outside it, ordering is
preserved and the subclass is still caught first.

Verification during the spike: every `atlas research provider-credential-*`
envelope byte-identical to a captured baseline, the 176-case envelope suite
green, `check_cli_command_compatibility.py` PASSED, and the converted module's
success paths exercised by `test_research_sandbox_cli.py`, which drives the real
CLI in process through 301 `main([...])` calls.

An earlier version of this entry also credited
`test_demo_research_workflow_script.py` with covering those success paths. It
does not: its 41 cases run against a *fake* `atlas` script to test the shell
workflow, so they would have passed whatever the spike did to the handlers. Its
36 subprocess spawns at ~4.2 s each are the shell script driving that fake, not
the CLI — which is what made the mistake easy to hold.

**That last line is the finding.** The acceptance criterion leans on
`test_research_command_envelopes.py` as the net for a 170-handler refactor, and
that test builds its argv from placeholder IDs and runs against an *empty*
workspace — so all 175 cases take the not-found path. It pins the error envelope
and the exit status, which is exactly what the wrapper absorbs, and it pins
**nothing about success output**. A migration that also rewrites success
branches, as the spike did, is covered only by whatever unrelated tests happen
to exercise a given command.

The size of that gap took two wrong measurements before a right one, which is
worth recording alongside the answer.

A scan for literal success statuses found **92 of 101 appearing somewhere in
`tests/`**, suggesting the worry was largely unfounded. It is not a measurement
of coverage. A status string appearing in a test file does not mean the suite
drives that command's success path — and for these commands it usually does not,
because their domain logic is tested by calling the functions directly while the
CLI layer is skipped.

Instrumenting `dispatch_research` over a full suite run measures the real thing.
Every one of the **170** commands executes; **153 reach exit 0 at least once**;
**17 never do**, so their success branch is never exercised:

| family | commands |
|---|---|
| `provider-safety-dossier` | base, `-export`, `-replay`, `-show`, `-summary`, `-validate` |
| `release-candidate-*` | `cutover-dry-run` + `-doctor`/`-summary`/`-validate`, `readiness-doctor`/`-show`/`-summary` |
| `sandbox` | `-replay`, `-show`, `-validate` |
| other | `market` |

The instrument only sees in-process dispatch, so a command exercised through a
real-CLI subprocess would be missed. Checked: it is not the case here.
`test_research_provider_safety_dossier.py` contains no `subprocess` usage at all
and imports the domain functions, and the one heavy subprocess test,
`test_demo_research_workflow_script.py`, spawns a *fake* `atlas` script rather
than the CLI.

So the prerequisite for the migration is 17 named commands whose success output
nothing pins — not 4, and not the whole surface.

#### The prerequisite, closed down to four

Those 17 were worked through, and the same instrument re-run after each step.
**169 of the 170 commands now reach exit 0.** The one that does not is `market`,
and it should not: `handle_market` returns 1 unconditionally, a legacy command
disabled in the frozen research pipeline with no success path to reach. It is
pinned as disabled, and the pin fails if it is ever re-enabled without one.

Getting there required correcting a wrong conclusion. The four cutover commands
were recorded here as unreachable from a temporary workspace, on the evidence
that creation answered `..._blocked` and so wrote no artifact for the inspection
commands to read. The cause was not repository state: `--target-version 9.9.9`
does not match `^v(\d+)\.(\d+)\.(\d+)-rc([1-9]\d*)$`, so it was refused before
any repository check ran. With `v0.6.27-rc1` the artifact is created and all four
reach exit 0, still reporting blockers — which is what a dry run is for.

The distinction that survives is narrower than the one first recorded. `-validate`
reaches exit 0 either way and answers `..._validation_failed`; it is the
`..._validated` *verdict* that needs the repository — README safety claims,
version consistency, a forbidden-claims scan, a clean protected boundary — not
the command. That constraint is real and is pinned at the domain level in
`test_release_candidate_readiness.py`.

Covered on the way: the three `sandbox` inspection commands, all six
`provider-safety-dossier` commands, and readiness `-show`/`-doctor`/`-summary`.
Every one had been uncovered for the same reason — its domain logic is tested by
calling the functions directly, so the CLI layer was reached only by the envelope
suite's placeholder ids on the not-found path.

Two findings came out of writing those tests. Two commands print the artifact
itself with no `ok`/`status` envelope (`provider-safety-dossier-show` and
`release-candidate-readiness-show`) and one reports `replay_hash_match` where its
sibling uses `match`, so the family is less uniform on the success side than on
the error side, and a wrapper must preserve that. And
`provider-safety-dossier-summary` names its positional `run_id` while the code
requires a dossier id, so following `--help` fails opaquely — see the open item
below.

**Where this leaves the candidate.** The safety net for a wrapper migration is now
real: 169 of 170 success paths are exercised, and the one exception has no success
path to exercise. What remains before landing is the migration itself, which is
mechanical but all-or-nothing.

Two things a migration must carry, both found by writing this coverage rather than
by reading the handlers. The family is not uniform on the success side:
`provider-safety-dossier-show` and `release-candidate-readiness-show` print the
artifact with no `ok`/`status` envelope at all, and `-replay` reports
`replay_hash_match` where its sandbox counterpart reports `match`. And
`provider-safety-dossier-summary` documents an argument it cannot accept, which is
recorded as its own open item.

The spike was deliberately not landed. Six of 170 converted would put two idioms
side by side, which this candidate already names as worse than the duplication.

#### Landed

The migration is complete. **`cli_commands/research/` went from 11,196 lines to
6,778 — 4,418 fewer, 39% smaller** — across 13 commits, one or two modules each,
because this environment reclaims uncommitted work and a 13-module change cannot
be held safely. All **170 handlers are on the new idiom and none on the old**, so
the acceptance criterion that every handler move rather than some is met with no
standing exception.

| | |
|---|---|
| `_envelope.py` | 137 lines, once, for all 170 handlers |
| Scripted conversions | 141 |
| Hand conversions | 29 |
| Handlers keeping a clause in the body | 29 |

The estimate before starting was −5,060 net lines; the outcome is −4,418. The
shortfall is where absorption was declined on purpose, which is the more useful
part of the result.

**What was not absorbed, and why.** Each of these keeps its clause inside the
handler body, with the reason at the site:

- **Prepended clauses on `ResearchSessionError` subclasses** (`InvalidResearchSymbolError`,
  `UnsupportedArtifactSchemaError`) and on unrelated `RuntimeError`s
  (`UnsupportedResearchProviderError`, `ResearchConfigurationError`). These are
  load-bearing: `safe_research_session_error` is an exact dict lookup on snake_case
  keys while these raise prose, so reaching the envelope would downgrade a specific
  status to `research_error`.
- **Clauses returning `2 if args.strict else 1`.** Canonical by type, not by
  behaviour. The envelope always returns 1.
- **Handlers whose only clause is `except Exception`.** The envelope would insert a
  `ResearchSessionError` clause ahead of it and map such an error to a specific
  status instead of `research_error`.
- **A clause returning `return 0`** (`handle_run`), where absorbing would change a
  success into a refusal.
- **Custom refusal envelopes.** `provider-plan-list` and `provider-execution-list`
  print their own invalid-symbol payload rather than calling
  `_research_error_json`.

**What the verification found that reading would not.** Two layers were used, and
they caught different things:

- The **envelope comparator** drove all 175 subcommands against a pre-migration
  baseline and reported 0 differences at every step. It covers refusal *shapes* on
  the `--json` path only.
- The **suite** caught both real defects. A first conversion script compared only
  the *types* of the two `except` clauses and absorbed one returning
  `2 if args.strict else 1`, silently changing the exit status; and wrapping
  `handle_providers` broke a test guarding its import surface, an axis I had not
  considered while checking a different one. Neither was visible to the comparator,
  and neither was in the quick gate's tier.

`test_cli_providers_handler_no_network_imports` was the only test in the suite
pinning the old idiom, and only as scaffolding: it asserted the dispatch-guard
string to confirm it had located the right block before scanning for banned
imports. The anchor moved to `list_research_providers`, then was re-checked by
planting `import httpx` and confirming the test still fails.

One correction to this entry's own earlier claim: `-replay` reporting
`replay_hash_match` was listed above as a divergence the migration must preserve.
It lives in `research/provider_safety_dossier.py`, not the handler, so the wrapper
never touched it.

Acceptance would require all 175 subcommands to keep the same envelope and exit
status, `scripts/check_cli_command_compatibility.py` to report no surface change,
and every handler to move rather than some — two idioms side by side would be
worse than the duplication.

This is CLI error handling and workspace resolution only: no gate added or
removed, and neither the risk gate nor the kill switch, approval queues, broker
adapters, or the audit hash-chain is touched. The fail-closed generic fallback
is preserved.

**Follow-up: the imports the scaffolding left behind, and a ratchet.** The
migration removed the `except` clauses but not the imports they needed. 177
function-scoped bindings of `ResearchSessionError`, `safe_research_session_error`
and `json` survived, referenced by nothing — invisible, harmless, and a standing
invitation to paste the clause back because the name is already in scope.
`handle_run` had also kept two clauses reproducing the envelope's behaviour
exactly, same status and message and exit code; its `ResearchConfigurationError`
clause stays, because that one returns 0.

Removing them brings the total to −4,614 lines. The envelope comparator reported
0 differences across all 175 subcommands after each pass. Two mechanical details
are worth recording, since both produced a wrong intermediate answer:

- The pruning script keyed dead bindings by name, so a function importing `json`
  twice had only the *second* statement recorded and the first survived. It took
  four passes to reach a fixed point. The ratchet below found the remainder; the
  script's own report of "158 removed, none left" was wrong.
- A `from x import A, B` on one line needs the statement regenerated rather than
  a line deleted. 28 bindings were skipped until that case was handled, and the
  script said so instead of silently leaving them.

`tests/architecture/test_research_handlers_use_the_shared_envelope.py` now pins
the result, because nothing else did: the research surface gains subcommands
often and the natural way to write the 171st handler is to copy the 170th. It
asserts no handler module reintroduces the dispatch guard, resolves its own
workspace, or emits `no_workspace`; that every registered handler carries the
decorator and that the decorator's argument matches its registry key; that only
five pinned handlers keep an error clause duplicating the envelope's; and that no
function-scoped import is left unreferenced. Each of the seven assertions was
checked by planting a violation and confirming the plant had applied to the file
before trusting the failure.

The prefix assertion is the one that changed character. Before the migration the
decorator's argument was the dispatch guard's comparison operand, so a mismatch
was unreachable code; now it is the command name printed in every error message
that handler can emit.

### CAND-037 — The per-artifact modules repeat their own helpers 83 times

Found while closing CAND-035, one layer down. `src/atlas_agent/research/` is
39,862 lines across ~30 `provider_*`/`release_candidate_*` modules, one per
artifact type, and each was written by copying its predecessor. Measured by
comparing the unparsed source of every module-level function, **83 copies are
byte-identical to another module's**:

| Helper | Copies | Variants |
|---|---|---|
| `_check_name` | 24 | 1 |
| `_get_disabled_provider_ids` | 16 | 2, differing only by an import a module does not need of itself |
| `validate_provider_id` | 21 | 14, each differing only in its error code |
| `validate_model_id` | 20 | 13, each differing only in its error code |
| `_is_inside_workspace` | 9 | 1 |
| `_has_unsafe_positive_claims` | 9 | 2, differing only by a docstring |
| `_build_broker_separation_policy` | 8 | 1 |
| `_MAX_MODEL_ID_CHARS` / `_MAX_STATUS_CHARS` | 20 / 16 | 1 each |

Structure-only clustering (node types and nesting, names and constants dropped)
finds more: 10 copies of a 78-line `validate_*_artifact`, 4 of an 82-line
`replay_*`, 3 of a 107-line `iter_*_artifacts`. The top twelve clusters are about
2,208 lines.

**What the copies were checked for first.** Duplication of a validator matters
because copies drift, and this project has already been bitten by that once —
`brokers/guards.py` held guards nobody called, and while nobody called them
`guard_sync` drifted into admitting `paper` as a live sync broker. So each family
was compared on substance before being counted as a refactor:

- All 20 `validate_model_id` copies agree: same 68-character allowed set, same
  120-character limit, same four checks in the same order. Only the error code
  differs, which is per-artifact by design. **No drift.**
- The variant counts above look alarming and mostly are not: the extra
  `_get_disabled_provider_ids` form omits an import of the module it lives in, and
  the extra `_has_unsafe_positive_claims` form omits a docstring.
- `_UNSAFE_POSITIVE_CLAIM_PHRASES` was the exception, and a real one. It is
  written up in
  [safety-invariant-audit-followups](../development/safety-invariant-audit-followups.md)
  and already fixed: nine lists, 85 phrases, empty intersection.

That is the useful shape of the result. Eight helper families were duplicated and
had not drifted; the ninth had, and it was the one whose copies were *not*
byte-identical. The measurement that found the refactor is the same one that found
the defect, which is the argument for doing the refactor: the ninth family drifted
because nothing compared the copies.

Acceptance would need the error code to stay per-artifact — passing it as an
argument, not unifying it — since 139 of these codes are already the CLI's
machine-readable vocabulary and CAND-034 is a separate decision. It would also
need the envelope-comparator equivalent for the domain layer: these functions are
reached through `atlas research`, so all 175 subcommands can be driven before and
after.

This is validator and artifact-loader plumbing: no gate added or removed, no
change to the risk gate, kill switch, approval queues, broker adapters, or the
audit hash-chain.
